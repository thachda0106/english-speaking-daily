# Day 15 · 📖 The Long Story — The Plan I Never Made

> One long story for the whole day. Read it out loud twice, shadowing the
> sentences you like. Then answer the speaking challenge below without stopping.

**I have a habit of saying yes to things and then not arranging them.** Not
refusing — just agreeing in the moment, in the way that stops a conversation
going badly, and then discovering on Saturday morning that I have nowhere to be
and no reason to leave the flat.

The thing I did not understand for years is that in English, agreeing is not the
same as committing. "Yeah, sounds good" is a thing you say so the moment can pass.
It is genuinely not a promise, and everybody who grew up speaking English knows
this without ever being told. I did not know it, because in my first language
saying yes means yes, and I have no idea how many times I have quietly
miscalculated.

My friend Duy was the one who finally explained it to me, and he did it by being
completely blunt. He said: "you always say yes to things you don't want to do."
Not unkindly — he said it the way you tell someone their chair is too low. And
then he taught me the actual sentence, which I now use constantly: "I was thinking maybe something low-key." Five words, and it does something saying yes cannot do, because it invites a conversation rather than closing one. If the other person says no, nothing has been lost. If they say yes, we have both agreed to something we worked out.

The test he gave me is the one I use now. When I say yes to something, I ask myself whether I would have suggested it myself. If the answer is no, then I have agreed out of politeness, and politeness is not free — it costs you the whole following week. I still say yes too often. But I now say "can we push it back?" sometimes, and "I can't this weekend" sometimes, and because I have said those two sentences enough times now that people don't blink when they hear them, they cost me nothing.

What I learned is that English has more than one way to agree, and a language that gives you more than one way has a decision hidden inside it. The question is never whether you said yes. The question is which yes you used.

## ✅ After you speak

**Formula:** *I have a habit of ___. The thing I did not understand is that ___. — My friend was blunt: "you always __." — The test I use now is ___. What I learned is that ___.

Reported speech with *"you always…"* keeps a friend's exact words, and past perfect for what you used to believe keeps the story ordered. Both make a small anecdote feel considered.
