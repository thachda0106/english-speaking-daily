# Day 19 · Conversation 1 — Saying What's Wrong

## 🎬 Situation
You're ill and you need to explain it. Most learners say *"I feel bad"* or *"I am not well, very"* — both too vague to be useful. English has a precise vocabulary for symptoms.

## 🗣️ Dialogue

> **Staff:** Hi, what can I do for you?

> **You:** I've got a headache and a bit of a temperature.

> **Staff:** Right. Any cough?

> **You:** A dry one. Mostly in the morning.

> **Staff:** And your throat?

> **You:** It's sore when I swallow. Otherwise it's fine.

> **Staff:** Sounds like a virus. Have you tried anything?

> **You:** Paracetamol, which helps for a few hours.

> **Staff:** Drink plenty of water and get some sleep. If it's not gone in a few days, see your doctor.

## 🧠 Your active vocabulary

- **I've got a headache** — **"I've got"** is how English says *"I have"* for illness and problems. **"I have headache"** is a common error
- **a bit of a temperature** — the British way of saying you have a fever. American: *"a fever"*
- **a dry cough** — the specific kind that doesn't produce anything. Wet cough is the other one
- **It's sore when I swallow** — describing exactly when it hurts. Doctors and pharmacists want this detail

## ✅ After you speak

**Formula:** *I've got a ___ and a bit of a ___. — It's ___ when I ___. — [what you've tried].*

⚠️ **"I've got" vs "I have":** both are correct English. The rule is that **illness and problems take "I've got"**, and everyone else takes *"I have"* — *I've got a cold · I have two brothers*. Using *"I have a headache"* is not wrong, it just sounds slightly formal.

## ✅ Done when…
- …you can name a symptom precisely rather than saying *"I feel bad"*
- …you can say when it hurts: *"it's sore when I swallow"*
- …you can say what you've already tried 🎯
