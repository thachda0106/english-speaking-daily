# Day 7 · 📖 The Long Story — The Day I Asked for More

> Every day has **one long story** that tells the whole day as a continuous narrative. Read it out loud twice, shadow the audio, then retell it in your own words — it trains connected speech, and today's story is full of **modals** (*could, would, should*) and the softer **"I would like"**.

---

**In the third month at my new company, I sat down one evening and wrote my salary on a piece of paper.**

The number was not bad, and I knew that — I had done my research before I signed. But three months of work later, it felt small. I compared it with the jobs I saw every day, and mine was at the bottom of the range. That made me feel stupid for a while, then angry, then tired.

I told nobody. For two weeks I said nothing, not even to my best friend. In my family nobody talks about money like that. Asking for more feels rude, like you are saying the work is not enough. I thought a good engineer should work well and wait.

The thing that changed me was not courage. It was preparation. I spent two evenings writing three numbers: what I earn now, what the market pays for a senior backend engineer with six years of experience, and the number I would say out loud. Then I read my last three months — the two services I own, the bug I found in month one. Those notes were facts, and they made me calm.

I still could not decide. Should I ask this month, or wait for the review at the end of the year? I asked an older friend. She said: you would like to be paid fairly — yes, so say it. Should you get more — probably yes. Would it be strange to ask — no, because every company expects you to ask. The person who asks with numbers is simply doing their job.

So I booked thirty minutes with my manager. I did not say "I want more money". I said, "I would like to talk about my salary. Here is my work for the last three months, here is what the market pays, and here is what I am asking for." Then I stopped talking, which was the hard part.

He was calm and kind, but he did not say yes to my number. The budget was fixed until next year, so he could not give the full increase. He could go from thirty million to thirty-three million, and he could move the review up by six months. That is what I got: three million, not ten.

I want to be honest about that part. For an hour I felt low, like I had made a mistake by asking. Then I read the new number and felt better. The company could not do more that year. That is a real limit, not a judgement of me.

Three days later my manager said, "When you joined, I had to read your code twice. Now I don't." That sentence was worth more than the three million. The money was never the first thing being decided. The trust came first, and the money was only the part both sides can write down.

Now, when I talk to a recruiter, I use the same three numbers: what I have now, what the market pays, and what I would like. If they cannot do more, I hear it and I do not argue. Asking is normal. Asking with facts is professional. And if the answer is no, I still know my own number.

---

## 🧠 Story vocabulary & grammar
- **Past-tense verbs you just heard/read:** `sat · wrote · signed · compared · felt · told · said · spent · found · asked · booked · stopped · read · joined`
- Today is about **modals**. *Could* is the weakest — "could we talk about…" is the safest way to open anything. *Would* is your polite offer — "I would like to talk about my salary". *Should* asks for advice or duty — "should I wait?". And **"I want" vs "I would like"** matters: *I want* sounds like a demand, *I would like* sounds like a professional request from one adult to another. Same meaning, very different room.
