# Day 22 · 📖 The Long Story — The Things I Quit

> One long story for the whole day. Read it out loud twice, shadowing the
> sentences you like. Then answer the speaking challenge below without stopping.

**I am good at starting things and bad at finishing them, and I have been both of
those for a very long time.** That is the short version. The longer version is
that I used to think the problem was discipline, and then I noticed that I
finish some things perfectly well, which means it wasn't discipline at all.

The list is not short. I gave up guitar twice, on the same guitar. I did four
months of Portuguese and then stopped, twice, which means the stopping was not
the accident I kept treating it as. I started a language app in January and
opened it in February. None of those things failed. They were working fine, and
then I stopped opening them, and that was the whole event.

**What changed was meeting someone who does the opposite.** A colleague of mine
has been doing the same three things for eleven years — running, piano, and a
language he is not going to be fluent in — and he has never once described any of
it as a phase. I asked him how he avoids the feeling of wanting something new,
and he said the most annoying sentence anyone has ever said to me about habits:
*"I don't get the urge any more, because I did it last Tuesday."*

That is the whole mechanism. The urge to stop was never about the thing being bad.
It was about a gap between two sessions being long enough for my attention to go
somewhere else. Now I do the thing, and the gap is four days instead of four
months, and there is no room in it for an urge.

The thing I was most surprised by is that I do not enjoy any of it more than
before. I have not become someone who likes running. I just do it, and I don't
have a strong feeling about it, which is a much quieter way to live than the
alternative, where every few months there is a week of feeling bad about
something I've stopped.

I still quit things. The difference is that I know why, and I know it lasts a
week rather than a year. There is a language app on my phone I have not opened
since June. I don't feel bad about it. That's the whole change.

What I learned is that the feeling of giving something up was never evidence
that I had given it up correctly.

## ✅ After you speak

**Formula:** *I am good at ___ and bad at ___. — The list is not short. — What changed was ___. — The thing I was most surprised by is that ___. What I learned is that ___.

Past perfect for the whole state — *"what I was most surprised by"* — keeps the reflection separate from the narrative. And note the last line is a reversal: it takes the thing the paragraph appeared to argue and flips it.
