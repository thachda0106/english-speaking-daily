# Day 26 · 📖 The Long Story — The Advice I Shouldn't Have Given

> One long story for the whole day. Read it out loud twice, shadowing the
> sentences you like. Then answer the speaking challenge below without stopping.

**I once told a colleague his approach was wrong, in a meeting, in front of six
people, and I was right about the code and completely wrong about the person.**
That second part took me two years to understand, and it is the only thing in
this story worth reading.

He had a design in front of us and I had been up until one in the morning
finding the flaw in it. There was one. It was a real one, and it would have cost
us a week later. And I said so, in the shape of a suggestion, but in the tone of
a verdict, and I did the thing I now know I do — I said "just to check" and then
delivered an argument.

He went quiet. Not defensive quiet. The other kind. He said "okay, fair" and
nothing else, for the rest of the meeting, and the meeting went fine, and nobody
noticed anything, and he resigned four months later.

**What I got wrong was the warning.** I had spent a night on the problem and he
had spent a month on it. When I stood up and undid a month of someone's work in
forty seconds because I had been up late, I was right about the code and I was
reckless about everything else. He never had a chance to have found it himself, and
in a small team that's most of what makes the work worth doing.

I did not put this in a report or in a review. I did not raise it privately
either. I told myself that going back over it would look like I was still trying
to win, which is not entirely untrue, and which is also a very convenient way to
avoid being told the same thing again.

What I did eventually do was ask him. He was already gone by then, so I asked
someone who had worked with him for years, and the answer was three sentences
long: *"I always knew you'd find it. I just wanted to find it myself first."*

That was the missing piece, and I could not have known it at the time, because
knowing it is what happens once you trust someone with a mistake. The warning was
not the problem. The warning was the *whole* problem — because it stopped him
doing the one thing that would have made the next time easier.

What I learned is that being right is the least interesting thing about being
right. What matters is whether the other person got to find it.

---

## ✅ After you speak

**Formula:** *I once told a colleague ___. What I got wrong was ___. — What I did eventually do was ___. That was the missing piece. What I learned is that ___.

Past perfect for the state before the mistake (*"I had been up until one"*, *"I had spent a month"*) makes the contrast land: two people's effort, side by side. And the ending reframes the whole story — the warning was right *and* was the problem.
