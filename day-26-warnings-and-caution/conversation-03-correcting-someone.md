# Day 26 · Conversation 3 — Correcting Someone, Gently

## 🎬 Situation
Someone has said something that's factually wrong, in front of other people. English has a way to correct without embarrassing them, and it's a mark of a careful speaker.

## 🗣️ Dialogue

> **Manager:** So we'll run it on the staging database first, and then on production.

> **You:** Just to check — staging is a copy of production, isn't it? So anything we run there will run again.

> **Manager:** That's the idea, yes.

> **You:** Okay. Then I'd check whether the migration's reversible before we do. Not because it won't work — because I'd rather know.

> **Manager:** You're right. That's a good point.

> **You:** Sorry, I didn't mean to slow it down.

> **Manager:** You're not slowing anything down. You're just asking the question I'd have asked.

## 🧠 Your active vocabulary

- **just to check —** — the softest possible opener for a correction. **"Just to check"** means *I'm asking, not telling you*, which changes everything
- **anything we run there will run again** — **"anything…will…"** describing a consequence, not accusing anyone
- **I'd rather know** — **"I'd rather"** puts the caution on you as a preference, not as a criticism of their plan
- **you're not slowing anything down** — the sentence that saves you from looking obstructive. Very useful

## ✅ After you speak

**Formula:** *Just to check — [the question]. — Then I'd check whether ___. Not because ___, because I'd rather know. — You're not slowing anything down.*

⚠️ **"Just to check" is the phrase that makes corrections acceptable.** A correction without it is an interruption. With it, it's a question — and everyone can answer questions without losing face. Native speakers use it constantly in meetings and almost never at home.

## ✅ Done when…
- …you can correct someone using *"just to check"*
- …you can separate the concern from the criticism
- …you can reassure with *"you're not slowing anything down"* 🎯
