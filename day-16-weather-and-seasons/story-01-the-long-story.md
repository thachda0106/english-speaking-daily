# Day 16 · 📖 The Long Story — The Year I Noticed Weather

> One long story for the whole day. Read it out loud twice, shadowing the
> sentences you like. Then answer the speaking challenge below without stopping.

**I did not notice the weather for about twenty-five years.** I am not
exaggerating. I lived in a place where the seasons barely changed, and where
people complained about heat in July and nothing at all the rest of the time, and
I was one of them. I would have told you, honestly, that I was not the sort of
person who notices weather. That it was something other people did.

Then I moved somewhere where it does that thing where the sky changes three times
before lunch, and I had to start paying attention for practical reasons. You
cannot leave the house in this city without checking, because you will be caught
out within the hour, and being caught out is a specific kind of miserable that
you only learn properly once.

What surprised me was not the weather. It was how much people talk about it. In
my first week here I sat in a café and heard two conversations that were
*entirely* about rain, and neither of them was about rain. One was about a
colleague who never brings an umbrella, and the other was about a wedding that
had been moved twice. Both of them were about people. The rain was the reason
they were talking and it was not the point.

A friend explained this to me and I've never forgotten the phrase, which is that
the weather in English is a door rather than a window. You don't talk about
meteorology. You use the weather to walk into everything else. And once you see
that, you realise how much of ordinary conversation is built that way — people
open with "it's freezing" and then, forty seconds later, tell you they're worried
about their mother.

So now I do notice it. It took a move to a place with real weather to teach me
that "how's the weather" was never actually a question about the sky. My first
year of speaking English would have found that depressing — all that vocabulary
thrown away on small talk. Now I think it's the most useful thing I learned. You
never have to think of something interesting to say. You can start with the sky,
and then walk into whatever you actually wanted to talk about.

What I learned is that in my first language the weather talk *was* the small
talk, because you'd already learned to say the thing directly. In English you
learn it as a detour, and once the detour is a habit, you can go anywhere in the
conversation through it.

## ✅ After you speak

**Formula:** *I did not ___ for about twenty-five years. What surprised me was not ___. It was ___. My friend explained this and I've never forgotten the phrase, which is that ___. What I learned is that ___.

Past perfect for a state that lasted — *"I did not notice for twenty-five years"* — and past simple for the moment you changed. That contrast carries the whole story.
