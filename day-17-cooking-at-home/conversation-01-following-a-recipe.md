# Day 17 · Conversation 1 — Following A Recipe

## 🎬 Situation
You're cooking from something you don't fully understand. English recipes use a small set of verbs, and they are not the ones you'd guess.

## 🗣️ Dialogue

> **Friend:** What are you making?

> **You:** Something with aubergine. I'm not sure it's going to work.

> **Friend:** Does it say what to do with the spices?

> **You:** It says "fold in" and I've no idea what that means.

> **Friend:** Just stir them in gently, at the end. That's all.

> **You:** "Fold in"? That's baking language.

> **Friend:** Recipes use baking words for everything. It says "season to taste" too, doesn't it?

> **You:** Yeah, and I think I used too much salt already.

> **Friend:** Add a bit more sugar and it'll balance out.

## 🧠 Your active vocabulary

- **fold in** — mix something in **gently**, usually at the end. Not the same as stirring hard
- **season to taste** — add salt and pepper until you like it, not according to a number
- **balance it out** — fixing one strong flavour with another. Extremely useful beyond cooking
- **Does it say what to do with ___?** — asking about a specific step, which is more useful than *"how do I cook this?"*

## ✅ After you speak

**Formula:** *It says "___" and I've no idea what that means. — Does it say what to do with ___?*

⚠️ **"To taste" means no measurement.** *"Season to taste"*, *"sugar to taste"*, *"salt to taste"* — all of them mean *add it until it's right*. If you see *"add 200g to taste"*, that's bad cooking writing.

## ✅ Done when…
- …you can follow a recipe without switching to another language
- …you can ask about one specific step: *"what does it want me to do with the ___?"*
- …you can use *"balance it out"* about anything, not just food 🎯
