# Day 17 · Conversation 4 — Feeding People

## 🎬 Situation
Someone's coming to eat and you're not sure you have enough. English has a whole set of phrases for this, and they're more social than logistical.

## 🗣️ Dialogue

> **Friend:** What time are you coming?

> **You:** About seven. There's loads — it's not a big deal.

> **Friend:** What do you want me to bring?

> **You:** Nothing, honestly. Maybe bread? Or wine.

> **Friend:** Sure. Anything you don't eat?

> **You:** Mushrooms. And I'm not a big fan of olives.

> **Friend:** Got it. Anything you're actually allergic to?

> **You:** No, nothing serious. I just don't like them.

> **Friend:** Right, that one's always worth asking.

## 🧠 Your active vocabulary

- **there's loads** — *"loads"*, *"loads of"*, *"plenty"* — British ways of saying *"a lot"*. **"There's loads"** is very natural
- **What do you want me to bring?** — the standard question when arriving somewhere
- **anything you don't eat?** — politeness, and not fussiness
- **Anything you're actually allergic to?** — **"actually"** is doing real work. It signals this is a different question

## ✅ After you speak

**Formula:** *About seven. There's loads. — Maybe bread? Or wine. — Anything you don't eat? — Anything you're actually allergic to?*

⚠️ **The distinction that matters:** *"I don't like olives"* is a preference. *"I'm allergic to olives"* is a safety warning. In English these are **completely different sentences**, and conflating them either minimises a real allergy or makes you sound dramatic about a dislike. **Say which one you mean.**

## ✅ Done when…
- …you can offer food without apologising for the amount
- …you can ask about preferences and allergies as two separate questions
- …you can use *"loads of"* and *"plenty"* for "a lot" 🎯
