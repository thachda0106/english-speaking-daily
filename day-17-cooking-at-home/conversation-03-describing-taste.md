# Day 17 · Conversation 3 — Describing Taste

## 🎬 Situation
Someone asks what you think of the food. Most learners can only say *"it's good"* — which gives the conversation nothing to work with.

## 🗣️ Dialogue

> **Friend:** Right, try it. What's it like?

> **You:** It's better than it looks, which is not the highest praise.

> **Friend:** Ha. Go on.

> **You:** It's a bit salty at the end, but the spices are actually good.

> **Friend:** Yeah? I wasn't sure about the cumin.

> **You:** Cumin's fine. It's more sour than I expected.

> **Friend:** Yeah, I was going for something sharp.

> **You:** It works. I just expected it to be milder.

> **Friend:** I'll add less lemon next time.

## 🧠 Your active vocabulary

- **It's better than it looks** — a backhanded compliment that's funnier than *"it's good"*
- **not the highest praise** — self-aware humour. Native speakers use understatement constantly
- **a bit salty / quite sharp / more sour than I expected** — **"a bit"** softens criticism, **"more ___ than I expected"** is honest without being rude
- **I'll add less ___ next time** — accepting feedback on your own cooking, which is a generous thing to say

## ✅ After you speak

**Formula:** *It's a bit ___ at the end, but the ___ are actually good. — It's more ___ than I expected.*

⚠️ **The useful pattern: "a bit X, but the Y is good."** It's a review that contains a real criticism and a real compliment. It sounds considered, and it gives the cook something to work with.

⚠️ **The mistake:** *"It's nice"* as a full stop. English speakers often say *"nice"* when they don't mean it. If you want to be honest, use *"it's fine"* (neutral) or *"it's not bad"* (mild praise) instead of *"nice"*.

## ✅ Done when…
- …you can describe a taste using three adjectives, not one
- …you can give a mixed review: *"a bit X, but the Y is good"*
- …you can avoid the empty *"it's nice"* 🎯
