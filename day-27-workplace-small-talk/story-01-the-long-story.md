# Day 27 · 📖 The Long Story — The First Month At The New Office

> Day 27 is the bridge between the two halves of this course. Days 1-10 were about getting work done; Days 11-26 were about talking about normal life. Work is both. This story is about the ordinary days in between — the parts nobody puts on a resume.

---

**The first month at my new office was the hardest month, and nothing interesting happened at all.**

Everyone warned me. My friend said the first month would be difficult. People online say the same thing — the first month is hard. I did not understand why, because I thought the hard part was the work. I already do backend work. I know databases. That part is not new.

The hard part was the small things. On my first morning I stood at the door for two minutes because I did not know where to put my things. My colleague said, "Morning! You must be the new person," and I answered too quietly. He did not hear me the first time. I said "hi" again, louder, and I felt strange about it, like I had done something wrong. I had only said hello.

The second week, someone asked me how I was in the kitchen. I said, "Fine." Then I stood there, because *fine* ends a conversation. My friend Ken looked at me and said, "You don't have to talk about work all the time." That was the most useful thing anyone said to me that month. I had been thinking of English as work only. It is not.

The third week, a colleague asked me about a part of the system I did not understand. I almost said "yes, I know" — I was afraid to say no. Instead I said, "I started it, but one part is hard for me right now. I don't know how the table is used, so I'm reading first." He said, "That sounds right. Do you want me to explain it?" We looked at it for ten minutes, and it was fine.

The last week, I finished my first real task. It was small. Someone asked how it was going, and I said, "Not bad. I finished one small thing, and it was easier than I thought — because I asked Ken in the morning." Ken said, "That's the trick, isn't it? Asking early saves the afternoon." I laughed, because it sounded like something a native person says, and I had never said it before.

At the end of the month I sat at my desk and noticed something. I still make mistakes. My English is not good. I still cannot find a word in the middle of a sentence, and I still say "thank you" when I should say "no, thank you." But nobody is surprised when I speak now. Nobody waits for me to finish. In week one, I thought everyone noticed every word. In week four, nobody was listening to my grammar at all — they were listening to my ideas.

**That was the real lesson of the first month. The grammar was never the problem. The fear was.** And fear is a habit, not a skill. It got smaller because I said hello out loud, again, every morning, until saying hello stopped being hard.

## 🧠 Story vocabulary & grammar
- **Past-tense verbs you just read:** `warned · was · thought · did · stood · know · said · heard · looked · asked · started · explained · finished · laughed · noticed · made · find · got`
- Notice the **linking words**: *because · so · but · until · and · when*. These are the glue of connected speech. Without them, English sounds like a list of separate facts. With them, it sounds like a person thinking.
- 🎯 **"Fine" is a trap.** One word, and the conversation is over. Try *"Not bad, thanks"* and you leave the door open. This is why fluent speakers almost never answer with one word.
